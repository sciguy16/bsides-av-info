    What the Real AI Attacks of the Last Two Years Actually Taught Us

    Many AI security talks deal in "an attacker could." This one deals only in what actually happened.

    In the last two years, real AI systems in production have leaked private data, deleted databases, been tricked through documents they were just supposed to read, and had their own connected tools turned against them. These are disclosed incidents and CVEs, not hypotheticals. And when you line them up, patterns emerge.

    This talk walks the real breaches that occurred in AI systems and pulls out the the common threads underneath: the same few root causes, over and over, in systems built by people who thought they'd been careful.

    I'll show what happened, why, and the handful of mistakes that keep repeating, so you can recognise them in your own stack before they're disclosed for you.

    If you want security for AI grounded in evidence, come see what the actual attacks can teach us.

    QUICly Changing Network Comms

    As defenses become more proactive and less reactive, the days of reliable unfettered egress to the internet are drawing to a close. At the same time, the increasing reliance on third parties necessitates increasing the size of the egress whitelist. The combination of the two requires a shift in C2 comms from relatively direct connections to infrastructure to proxying data via whitelisted services. Unfortunately, however, whitelisted services are often somewhat reluctant to get involved in even a simple shell-over-TLS connection, instead insisting on REST APIs, bespoke protocols, or worse.

    Fortunately for those of us who really would like to receive encrypted shell output, it turns out all we really need is something which can transport discrete messages from compromised hosts back to our own infrastructure, at the cost of having to deal with session management, timeouts, in-order delivery, and all of the other fiddly details TCP and TLS solve. Even more fortunately, there's a protocol which translates discrete messages to something TCPish: QUIC.

    We'll take a look at (ab)using QUIC, the protocol underlying HTTP/3, to take care of some of the complexity of sending a TLS stream over nearly anything that can shuffle messages between our shell and our server. We'll first take a high-level look at QUIC itself, drawing parallels to TCP and to SSH, and then we'll take a look at the fun bits of a simple implant using shell I/O over TLS over QUIC over SaaS.

    While the talk will make use of the programming language [Go](https://go.dev), no prior experience with Go will be assumed. Some familiarity with programming will be of benefit to understand the finer details of the talk, but no programming background will be necessary to benefit from the overarching concepts.

    45,724 Networks, One Flipper Zero: Mapping the Wireless Threat Landscape of Northern Ireland

    Nearly one in ten WiFi networks across Northern Ireland is still advertising WPS - a protocol the Wi-Fi Alliance deprecated in 2012 due to well-documented cryptographic weaknesses. Most network owners have no idea.

    This talk presents the findings of an independent, passive wireless survey of 45,724 networks across Northern Ireland, conducted using commodity hardware: a Flipper Zero with an ESP32-S2 WiFi Dev Board running Marauder firmware, paired with Kismet and an Alfa adapter. No networks were accessed, probed, or interacted with - every data point was gathered from publicly broadcast beacon frames using a strictly passive methodology compliant with the Investigatory Powers Act 2016, the Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006, and the Computer Misuse Act 1990.

    The numbers tell a stark story. Of the 45,724 networks observed, 4,460 (9.75%) were actively advertising WPS, and 555 (1.2%) were completely open with no encryption at all. The A26 corridor alone - a single arterial route - yielded 12,730 networks in one survey pass. Default SSIDs from major ISPs were widespread, suggesting factory configurations left untouched. Affected networks were identified near healthcare facilities, schools, legal practices, financial services offices, and government buildings.

    The talk walks through three realistic attack scenarios grounded in these findings. First, WPS PIN brute-force against a healthcare-adjacent network using CVE-2011-5053 (the Viehbock half-PIN weakness that reduces the keyspace from 100 million to roughly 11,000 combinations) and CVE-2014-9494 (Pixie Dust offline recovery). Second, passive eavesdropping on open networks near educational institutions. Third, evil twin deployment targeting default-SSID networks around financial services. Each scenario is mapped to real-world precedent - from the TJX breach to the GRU close-access WiFi operations documented in the 2018 OPCW indictments.

    I will also present a case study from my own bug bounty research: a WPS PIN vulnerability discovered in BMW in-vehicle hotspots broadcasting persistently from production vehicles on public roads. The affected access points were detectable at over 100 metres using the same commodity hardware - more than 33 times the range BMW's own documentation states the feature should operate at. This finding, submitted through a responsible disclosure programme, demonstrates that the WPS problem extends beyond fixed infrastructure into the automotive IoT space.

    The session closes with a sector-by-sector risk assessment for Northern Ireland and a set of practical, actionable recommendations: immediate steps (disable WPS, close open networks), short-term measures (network segmentation, WPA3 migration, 802.1X), and strategic changes (regulatory enforcement, public awareness). Every recommendation is grounded in the data presented.

    Attendees will leave with a clear understanding of how easy it is to map wireless infrastructure at scale using cheap, legal, passive tools - and why the results should concern anyone responsible for network security in Northern Ireland or similar regions. No specialist equipment or deep wireless expertise is required to follow along.

    OWASP PTK: Security Testing Where the Browser Is the Source of Truth

    Modern web applications are no longer simple request-and-response systems. They are authenticated, JavaScript-heavy, stateful, SPA-driven, and full of behaviour that only appears after a real user follows the right path through the browser. Traditional scanners and proxy-based workflows are powerful, but they can lose important context: the current user session, DOM state, client-side routes, tokens, storage, runtime JavaScript behaviour, and the exact sequence of actions that produced a finding.

    This talk introduces OWASP PenTest Kit, a browser-native application security toolkit designed to bring security testing closer to where modern applications actually run. I will explain why PTK exists, the problems it tries to solve, and how browser-based testing can complement established AppSec workflows rather than replace them.

    The session will cover practical PTK workflows across DAST, SAST, IAST, and browser-driven scanning, showing how testing from inside the browser can help with authenticated flows, single-page applications, client-side code, request inspection, tokens, cookies, and live application behaviour. I will also cover how PTK integrates with OWASP ZAP, combining ZAP’s proxy and traffic visibility with PTK’s in-browser analysis and testing capabilities.

    The second half of the talk focuses on automation. I will show how PTK can be used beyond manual testing through its npm package, crawler-driven workflows, and support for agent-assisted approaches using tools such as OpenCode and Codex-style automation. The goal is to demonstrate how browser-native testing, crawling, automation, and existing proxy tooling can work together in a repeatable AppSec workflow.

    Attendees will leave with a practical understanding of where OWASP PTK fits, what problems it is designed to solve, how it can support manual and automated testing, and how browser-native security tooling can improve coverage for modern web applications.

    From Code to Coverage: A Detection Engineer's Journey Through the LDAP Wilderness

    Active Directory reconnaissance tools like BloodHound, Impacket, and SOAPHound are the attacker's first move in enterprise compromises, yet detecting their LDAP queries remains one of the hardest problems in security operations. This talk chronicles a six month journey from writing my first broken Sigma rule to building a complete, evasion resistant LDAP detection stack.

    You'll learn why traditional signature based detection fails spectacularly, how to think like both an attacker and a parser, and how mathematical approaches can outsmart evasion techniques. We'll cover OID transformations that break your rules, whitespace variations that mock your regex, hidden LDAP parameters that bypass your detections, and ultimately, statistical methods that make evasion mathematically impossible.

    This isn't theory. Every technique is battle tested in production environments with working Sigma rules, real attack logs, and actual false positive rates. Leave with detection rules you can deploy Monday morning.

    Reverse Engineering an OAuth Supply Chain Attack

    Let's reconstruct an OAuth supply chain attack.

    In these attacks no one really breaks into your company. The attackers simply walk through the door, using trusted credentials you've given to one of your vendors. They are impossible to prevent. But, is there really nothing we can do about it?

    The best way to figure it out is to do it yourself: so, let's create 3 organisations, give them all real infrastructure, and reverse engineer a real breach to see what allows supply chain attacks to be most effective.

    We'll design potential attacks (incl. LLM-assisted attacks), run them against real-world infrastructure, and find out what makes breaking in easier (or, harder) when a trusted vendor is breached.

    This talk is based on the Vercel breach in April 2026.
    https://vercel.com/kb/bulletin/vercel-april-2026-security-incident

    ---

    Earlier this year we got a breach notification from Vercel. The kind I've had to write myself in the past. This time I was on the other end.

    "We’ve identified a security incident that involved unauthorized access to certain internal Vercel systems."

    The email said we're (probably) not affected. But, the public information they shared got me thinking about the blanks they left out.

    1. What really happened?
    2. How can the access we grant 3rd-parties as part of standard day-to-day work be abused?
    3. Can an upstream breach move through our company, and propagate downstream our customers?
    4. What is the real risk?
    5. Can we do anything about it?

    I decided to figure it out.

    So, I built the infrastructure for 3 organisations using real services and realistic architectures. Then, experimented with designing ways to break in. The results were practical insights for defence - finding out what makes these attacks easy, and what makes them hard.

    That's what I'd like to share in this talk, with a (hopefully) interactive demo of a (demonstrative) OAuth supply chain attack.

    Inside the CSIRT: A Collaborative Live Incident Response Workshop

    Incident response frameworks tell us what responders should do, but how do security practitioners actually investigate and respond to an attack when working under pressure?

    This handson workshop places participants inside a realistic simulated ransomware incident, working as part of a Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT). In small groups, attendees will investigate, contain, and respond to an attack using open source incident response tools including TheHive (Case Management), MISP (Threat Intelligence), Cortex (Enrichment), and Mattermost (Communication).

    Following a guided introduction and training session, participants will be presented with a realistic ransomware scenario. Teams will analyse indicators of compromise, document response activities, share threat intelligence, coordinate decisions, and manage the incident through the phases of the NIST Incident Response lifecycle. Participants will work collaboratively while following their own investigative workflow and response strategy.

    Participants will:
    -Learn the fundamentals of incident management using TheHive, MISP, Cortex, and Mattermost.
    -Experience a realistic ransomware investigation from initial detection through analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, and post-incident activities.
    -Practise documenting actions using the NIST Incident Response framework.
    -Analyse indicators of compromise using threat intelligence and enrichment tools.
    -Collaborate with team members to make critical response decisions throughout the incident lifecycle.

    No prior experience with TheHive, MISP, or Cortex is required. The workshop is suitable for students, SOC analysts, blue team practitioners, incident responders, and anyone interested in cybersecurity operations. Attendees will leave with practical experience using modern incident response platforms, a deeper understanding of ransomware response workflows, and insight into how incident responders make decisions during real world investigations. Unlike traditional training exercises, this workshop also contributes to an ongoing cybersecurity research study exploring how different responders approach the same incident.

    Chaining the Unchainable: Finding and Exploiting Logic Flaws in Modern Web Architectures

    Most scanners will never find these bugs. This talk is about what happens when you chain enough of them together anyway. Spoiler: it ends in RCE and full tenant isolation bypass.

    Business logic flaws are unglamorous. They don't have CVEs, they don't get blog posts, and individually they'll barely register as medium severity on most reports. But in the microservice-heavy, federated identity, multi-tenant environments most organisations are running today, these small mismatches compound. The interesting stuff happens at the joins, like where an API gateway hands off to a downstream service, where an event queue trusts state it shouldn't, or where OAuth flows have one assumption too many baked in.

    This session walks through three attack chains pulled from real engagements. This is not sanitised theory. We will look at actual proxy logs, HTTP pairs, and the exact moment each system broke. The three paths covered include:

    1. Race conditions in async queues: Abusing state validation gaps in event-driven architectures to push through unauthorised transactions.

    2. Token propagation abuse: Exploiting the trust gap between an API gateway and its downstream services to chain into deep IDOR.

    3. OAuth state-machine quirks: Turning minor implementation oversights into persistent session hijacking.

    The goal isn't just to show what broke. Attendees will come away with a method for reading an architecture diagram and spotting where the logic synchronisation is weakest, a framework for building these chains deliberately, and concrete design-layer fixes that actually address the root cause rather than just patching symptoms.

    Key Takeaways for Attendees:

    - A clear method for analyzing an architecture diagram to spot where logic synchronization is most vulnerable.

    - An adversarial framework for systematically building exploit chains out of low-severity context flaws.

    - Concrete, design-layer remediation strategies that address the root cause of these architectural gaps.

    Bedtime Bug Hunting: A Friendly Android Auth Bypass Story

    One night, while lying in bed and idly poking around an Android app for a membership organisation I belong to, I found myself somewhere I did not expect to be: past the login flow.
    This lightning talk is a short, friendly story about curiosity, mobile apps, and responsible disclosure. It is not about shaming an organisation or showing off a live exploit. In fact, once the issue was reported, the organisation responded quickly and the app was patched soon after. That is the kind of outcome we should want to see more often.
    The talk will cover the discovery at a high level: what caught my attention, how I tested the behaviour safely, what the app appeared to trust, and why authentication and authorisation need to be enforced server-side rather than relying on client-side flows or assumptions. I will also talk about where I stopped, what I avoided doing, how I documented the finding, and how I approached reporting it constructively.
    The broader theme is the value of a playful security mindset. Many useful findings start with the simple question: “What happens if I press this?” Makers, engineers, testers, and defenders all benefit from gently poking at systems, noticing odd behaviour, and then handling what they find with care.
    Attendees will come away with a better sense of how mobile authentication flows can go wrong, what good responsible disclosure can look like, and why curiosity plus ethics is such a useful combination in security.

    Leaks on the Livewire: How a Few Blocked Requests Led to a Shocking Discovery

    What started as unremarkable PHP deserialization attacks blocked by our Cloud WAF unravelled to reveal a sprawling credential theft operation. This talk walks through the full lifecycle of our investigation into exploit attempts against CVE-2025-54068 (a critical unauthenticated RCE in Laravel Livewire v3), from initial detection to recovery and analysis of the attacker's exfiltration infrastructure.

    We'll cover the vulnerability mechanics, the PHP gadget chains used to exploit it, and the credential-stealing malware deployed post-exploitation. The real story, however, lies in what came next: gaining access to the attacker's C2 infrastructure and analysing over 21,000 stolen Laravel .env files.

    A single .env file contains the keys to an entire application: database credentials, payment processor secrets, cloud access keys, and encryption keys. The scale of what we found across thousands of compromised applications may surprise you, as will the OSINT trail that led us to the threat actor. We close with practical detection and mitigation guidance and a clear-eyed look at what .env exposure really means for an organisation.
